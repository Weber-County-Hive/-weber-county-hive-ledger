Who's Asking?

The short, simple version. No tables.

Posted Nov. 17, 2026 · Updated Nov. 17, 2026

Want every number and every source?Read the full case file →

The big idea

Polls used to be phone calls. Now many are texts with a link, or online surveys people sign up for. Campaigns use voter lists and other data to find you for polls, ads, texts and mail. Here is what's public about you, what the law allows, and what you can do.

Your voter record is mostly public again

$1,050Anyone can buy Utah's statewide voter list since May 25, 2026
1.3 millionVoters lost the choice to keep their record private, unless they qualify as "at-risk"
  • Public: your name, address, party, voter ID number, and which elections you voted in.
  • Always private: your Social Security number, driver license, full birth date, phone, email and signature.
  • Never public: how you voted.
  • Starting in 2027, the form asks if you'll share your phone and email with your party. You can say no.

The state also sells a daily list of who has already voted, for the 21 days before Election Day. It shows that you voted, not how.

How Utah got here

  • 2018: any voter could make their record private.
  • 2020: parties and candidates could see private records. People at risk, like domestic violence victims, got extra protection.
  • 2026: a new law (S.B. 153) ended private status for most voters. People at risk can still apply.
  • Nov. 18, 2026: a legislative committee looks at a draft to change who counts as at-risk.

How polls changed

  • Most national polls now use the internet. Before the 2020 election, 84% did.
  • Some online polls pick people at random. Others use people who signed up, so the results are adjusted.
  • Polls got closer to the final results in 2024 than in 2020.
  • A "push poll" is not a real poll. It's a sales pitch dressed up as one: one or two questions, all negative, and no one will say who's calling.
  • In Utah, a poll about a candidate or a ballot measure must tell you who paid for it, by the end of the call. The fine for not telling you is $100.

Did you sign up for that text?

  • The Do Not Call list doesn't stop campaign calls or texts.
  • Texts sent by a machine to cell phones need your OK. Some texts sent by a person don't.
  • The spam-email law doesn't cover political email.
  • Utah's "paid for by" rule doesn't name texts or social media.

What you can do

  • Reply STOP to campaign texts. Forward spam texts to 7726.
  • On a Facebook ad, tap the three dots and choose "Why am I seeing this ad?"
  • Look up a candidate's ads in the Meta Ad Library.
  • Check your own voter record at votesearch.utah.gov.
  • If you're at risk, ask your county clerk about at-risk status.

When tax money paid for a survey and ads

A housing group called UWHA got $2 million from the Legislature. It paid for research, made ads, and used the ads to collect more survey answers. One $62,500 payment shows up in its first report to the state but we didn't find it in the final one. That doesn't prove anything was wrong, but it's a question to ask. More in Case 02.

Our grade: D

Campaigns report who they paid, which is the minimum the law asks. But voters have less say over their own data, and they can't see who is targeting them or why.

What you can ask

  • Your lawmakers (le.utah.gov): Should campaign texts have a do-not-text option? Should "paid for by" cover texts?
  • The state elections office (vote.utah.gov): Who bought the voter list in 2026?
  • Pollsters: Who ran this poll, who paid for it, and was it random or opt-in?
Fair to all: Paying a pollster or a texting company is legal and common in every party. A public voter list also helps new candidates and news reporters. These records show what's public and who got paid. They don't show who was targeted.
