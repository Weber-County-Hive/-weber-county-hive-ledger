Who Gets Paid to Sell You a Candidate

Billboards, mailers, signs and the firms that run campaigns. One billboard company gave $183,276 to 2026 Utah campaigns, and campaigns paid it $93,725.73. Here is every line, from the state's own records.

Posted Nov. 17, 2026 · Updated Nov. 17, 2026 · Figures as of Nov. 17, 2026

In plain words

Most of the money a campaign raises ends up with someone who sells marketing: billboards, mailers, yard signs, digital ads, signature gatherers and the consultants who plan it all. Every one of those payments has to be reported. So does every gift a marketing company makes to a candidate.

A tip came in with two tables about one billboard company, Reagan Outdoor Advertising. We checked both against the state's campaign finance site, disclosures.utah.gov, line by line, and found eight more entries the tables missed.

What the records show: In 2026, Reagan gave $183,276 to Utah candidates, PACs and parties, and campaigns paid Reagan $93,725.73, almost all for advertising. Eight campaigns are on both lists. Those are two different kinds of money, so we never add them together. The records don't show prices, how many boards each campaign got, or whether every candidate was offered the same terms.

2. Eight campaigns on both lists

Why these eight? These are the campaigns we identified in both our 2026 Reagan contribution and expenditure searches. We matched every recipient name on the contribution list against every payer name on the confirmed expenditure list, and these eight are the only matches. A ninth, Trevor Lee, received a $1,000 gift from Reagan and paid $220.10 to a payee filed only as "Reagan"; we list him as unconfirmed. Inclusion shows a donor–vendor relationship; it does not establish preferential pricing, coordination or wrongdoing.

Cash or donated space? The state's 2026 year files mark each gift. Of Reagan's 70 gifts, 14 ($49,676) are marked in-kind, described as billboards, donated space or advertising, and 56 ($133,600) are cash. For seven of these eight campaigns, Reagan's gift was donated billboard space or advertising, not cash; the campaign also paid Reagan for other advertising. Douglas Fiefia's $1,000 gift was cash. An in-kind gift can work like a discount, so a gift and a payment to the same campaign are not two separate cash transactions.

What the reports don't show

A campaign report shows the payment. It doesn't show the rate, how many boards, or whether another candidate was offered the same terms. Some reports list in-kind gifts from billboard companies, meaning donated space instead of cash. In June we documented one: a Weber County Commission campaign (Sharon Bolos) reported a $1,000 in-kind gift from Reagan and a $4,950 payment to Reagan on the same day, May 7, 2026. As the candidate pointed out at the time, an in-kind gift can work as a discount, so the campaign's net cost may have been lower than the payment alone.

Past public reporting

In 2012, KSL reported that Reagan turned down a Davis County commission candidate's billboard about commissioner pay. Dewey Reagan called it negative campaigning. KSL also reported that an earlier voicemail to the candidate from "Reagan's Marc Davey" gave another reason: the commission was one of the company's landlords. Dewey Reagan told KSL the decision had nothing to do with supporting one candidate over another (KSL, May 31, 2012). In 2015, news outlets reported that Bill and Dewey Reagan ran a PAC, Utahns for Independent Government, that put up billboards for every Salt Lake City mayoral candidate except the incumbent (Utah Policy, Fox 13, KSL, 2015).

In July 2024, Scenic Utah, a nonprofit led by former Salt Lake City Mayor Ralph Becker, sent the Federal Highway Administration and UDOT a list of 29 billboards across the state it says are illegal or nonconforming, including a set of Reagan boards along I-15 in Orem. As of that report, neither agency had found any of them illegal (Deseret News / KSL, July 2024). In 2024, a Reagan Outdoor lobbyist, Nate Sechrest, spoke for a bill on billboards along scenic byways, which Scenic Utah opposed (Salt Lake Tribune, Jan. 15, 2024). These are disputes over where boards stand, not over campaign ads; we include them because they show the company's business is shaped by state law and state agencies.

3. The rules: TV must sell to both sides. Billboards don't have to.

Federal law treats broadcast stations differently from billboard companies. If a TV or radio station lets one legally qualified candidate use its airtime, it must give equal opportunities to the other candidates for that office, and it has no power to censor what they say (47 U.S.C. § 315(a)). No federal law puts that duty on outdoor advertising.

At least one state does. Since 1965, Minnesota law has made it unlawful for an outdoor advertising company to discriminate in providing space based on "race, color, creed or political affiliation." The company still doesn't have to sell to anyone unwilling to pay its rates (Minn. Stat. § 325E.24). Utah has no similar law that we found.

Utah lawmakers have taken up billboards and signs in recent sessions:

  • H.B. 198 (2025), "Highway Expansion Impacts on Signage Amendments," sponsored by Rep. Val Peterson. It changed the rules for moving or raising billboards when road work blocks them. A summary of the Senate Economic Development and Workforce Services Committee hearing records a Reagan Outdoor Advertising representative speaking in support. Reagan gave Rep. Peterson $1,500 in 2026.
  • H.B. 33 (2026), "Political Signs Amendments," primary sponsor Rep. Jordan Teuscher, Senate sponsor Sen. Evan Vickers. The bill as introduced required a political sign's "paid for by" disclosure to be "reasonably legible" to someone standing near the sign. The version that passed kept a readability rule only for billboards (text "large enough to be easily readable by an ordinary observer at a reasonable distance") and dropped it for ordinary signs. It also shrank the exemption for small signs from 4 by 8 feet to under 24 by 18 inches, so more signs will need a "paid for by" line. Those changes take effect Jan. 1, 2027 (le.utah.gov, H.B. 33 introduced and enrolled). Reagan gave Sen. Vickers $1,000 and Conservative Millennials PAC, whose officers include Rep. Teuscher, $1,000 in 2026.
  • 2027: no bill requests had been filed under the Legislature's "Signs and Billboards" subject as of Nov. 17, 2026 (le.utah.gov, 2027 General Session).

4. Mailers: who's paying for what lands in your mailbox

State law requires a mailed political ad to say who paid for it. If no candidate authorized it, it must also say that (Utah Code 20A-11-901).

  • A candidate's own mailer. A reader sent us a 2026 mailer from Speaker Mike Schultz's House District 12 campaign. Its fine print reads "Paid for by Elect Mike Schultz."
  • A mailer with no clear payer. In October, voters in Senate District 21 received a mailer attacking Sen. Brady Brammer with a return address of "10,000 Integrity Trackers." No "paid for by" line was visible on the copy we saw, and we found no political committee, issue committee or corporation filed under that name (disclosures.utah.gov, searched Oct. 24, 2026).
  • Postage through a party permit. House District 10 candidate Jim Alvey's reports list $10,210 paid to "State Inicia" in three payments (Sept. 21 and Oct. 13, 2026), with no address. Doug Fiefia's reports list $12,444 to "Utah Republican Party State Indicia," the name of the party's program for candidate mailers on its nonprofit postage permit. In 2026, Republican candidates and PACs reported paying the party $441,267.67, and the party reported 39 payments to USPS totaling $305,345.98. The report lines don't name the Utah Republican Party as the payee, so a reader can't see the party from the line alone, and no filing on either side uses the word "postage." Each payment shows the candidate's own campaign paid for the mailing. Full details: The State Party Indicia (Unelected Power, UP 100.13).
  • The same postage line in 2025 city races. In Riverton, a mayoral candidate's mailer said "Postage paid by the Utah Republican Party" but did not say who paid for the mailer or whether the candidate authorized it; the Lieutenant Governor's Office said that omission broke state law and sent its standard notice. The candidate said she reimbursed the party for the postage. Utah GOP Chair Robert Axson said candidates must request the party's discounted rate and donate the full mailing cost to the party (Salt Lake Tribune, July 31, 2025). In Provo, the mayor's postcards carried the same "Postage Paid by" line, and her reports listed just under $1,400 paid to the party for postage and a small donation (KUER, Oct. 23, 2025). The party's candidate page lists a "Postage Discount Form" and says party resources after convention go only to candidates who won at convention.
  • Missing lines are common, and the fix is a notice. The Lieutenant Governor's Office called a missing "paid for by" line on a mailer "a fairly common mistake" and said it has a standard procedure to notify the candidate (Salt Lake Tribune, Aug. 6, 2025, Lehi; Aug. 7, 2025, Hurricane). The notice reminds the candidate that full compliance is expected going forward; the news reports describe no fine.

5. Negative and unsigned mailers

Utah's attribution rule covers mailers, billboards, broadcast, print and "any other type of general public political advertising." An ad must clearly say who paid for it. An ad a candidate didn't authorize must also say it "is not authorized by any candidate or candidate's committee." Through 2026, lawn signs up to 4 by 8 feet, bumper stickers, pins and buttons are exempt (Utah Code 20A-11-901). Starting Jan. 1, 2027, H.B. 33 shrinks the sign exemption to signs smaller than 24 by 18 inches, or a candidate's own sign that names the candidate. The rule is about who paid. It doesn't check whether what the ad says is true.

Texts, social media and AI images

The same groups that mail also text. Preserving Utah Values (2024) and Utahns for Safer Communities (2026) both sent texts as well as mail. On the campaign side, texting shows up as a vendor payment. In the state's 2026 files, 17 campaigns and committees paid VOTTIV (filed under several spellings) $52,796.21, and 15 more paid other texting services such as Scale to Win, RumbleUp, Peerly and Prompt.io $16,502.33. In county races, Sharon Bolos's 2026 Weber County report lists $1,859.81 to VOTTIV Campaigns for texts.

  • Utah's "paid for by" rule reaches "any other type of general public political advertising," but it doesn't name text messages, social media or influencer posts.
  • H.B. 112 (2026), primary sponsor Rep. Sahara Hayes, Senate sponsor Sen. Ronald Winterton, would have added paid internet and social media ads to the rule and let election officers fine violators ($1,000 per ad in statewide and legislative races, $500 in local ones) instead of relying on criminal charges. It passed the House 66-1 and failed 2-3 in a Senate committee on March 3, 2026.
  • November 2025, Park City. On the eve of the city election, a text titled "GOP Voting Alert" appeared to back mayor and council candidates in nonpartisan races. The Summit County Republican Party said it made no endorsements, that the text went mainly to Democratic voters, and that it had no "paid for by" line. The party filed a complaint asking the Lieutenant Governor's Office to find out who paid for and sent it (TownLift, Nov. 17, 2025). We have not found a public outcome. The mayor's race was decided by 11 votes in unofficial results.
  • AI images. Since May 1, 2024, a Utah political ad that uses AI-made images, video or audio must say so, for example "This image generated by AI" (S.B. 131, 2024).
  • June 2026, Senate President Stuart Adams's primary. Make Liberty Win, a PAC funded by Young Americans for Liberty, put $100,000 into mailers, texts and calls. Its state filing lists $137,500 from Make Liberty Win's federal PAC on May 18, payments to Creative Influence ($59,676.63), Prompt.io (a texting platform) and Traction Control, and $60,152.70 returned to the federal PAC on July 10; at least five mail pieces showed AI-generated images of Adams (Deseret News, June 11, 2026). The article doesn't say whether the AI label appeared. On the other side, Salt Lake Indivisible sent about 6,000 postcards urging voters to oust Adams with no "paid for" line naming the group; an organizer said volunteers paid for most of it. Adams's campaign asked the Lieutenant Governor's Office to investigate.

Mike Schultz and outside ads, before 2026

Records show House Speaker Mike Schultz paid for outside advertising through PACs in earlier elections:

  • 2022: Utahns for Safer Communities' reports list $25,000 and then $100,000 from Mike Schultz. The PAC paid Hartley Consulting for advertising, then Chase Marketing Group $99,657 for advertising from Sept. 29 to Oct. 21, 2022. The reports don't say who the ads were about.
  • 2024: Preserving Utah Values PAC sent mailers and texts attacking Democratic lawmakers. A later report showed Schultz gave it $120,000. Schultz said in a statement that defending Utah values "is the right thing to do" (Salt Lake Tribune, Dec. 2, 2024).

Giving to a PAC that runs independent ads is legal. We report what the filings show; they don't show who wrote or approved the ads.

Timing has come up before. In 2020, lobbyists Spencer Stokes and Casey Hill and organizer Karen Hammond formed Utah Term Limits, a PAC that sent mailers in two state Senate Republican primaries. The Salt Lake Tribune reported it was formed one day after the deadlines that would have required it to disclose donors and spending before the primary, so donors would not show until a report due after the election (Salt Lake Tribune, June 26, 2020). That is the same pattern seen in 2024 and 2026: the ad arrives first and the money shows up in a later report.

Roy City Citizens: still waiting

In Roy, the "Vote FOR" mailer on the Prop. 10 tax referendum came from Roy City Citizens. Its organizer, Randy Scadden, told the Hive in writing on Oct. 24, 2026, that he is the group's only funder, and the Hive said it would report it that way until the group files its report. As of Nov. 17, 2026, we have no update and have not seen the group's report. The full exchange is in the Roy case file.

What the record shows across these cases: the rules require a name on the ad and a report with the state, but the money behind an attack can surface after voters have already received it, or under a name that doesn't say who's behind it. When the Lieutenant Governor's Office got complaints about a PAC reporting $0, its stated next step was to try to contact the PAC and then forward the complaint to the Attorney General (Salt Lake Tribune, Dec. 4, 2024).

How the rules could better protect the public

  1. Show totals and group spellings on disclosures.utah.gov, and show cash vs. in-kind in search results, not only in the year files.
  2. Require a rate disclosure when a vendor that gives to a campaign is also paid by it, or require one published political rate for every candidate, as federal law already requires of broadcasters (47 U.S.C. § 315(b)).
  3. Add a readable "paid for by" rule for every sign that must carry one, not only billboards.
  4. Require a PAC to report its funders before its ads go out, not at the next deadline.
  5. Cover texts and social media in the "paid for by" rule, as H.B. 112 proposed in 2026.
  6. Name the real sponsor on outside mailers, such as the top donors, so a name like "Integrity Trackers" or "Republicans Against" can't hide who paid.

These are policy options drawn from what the records show is missing. They are not endorsements of any bill.

What this search can't see

  • Cash vs. donated space. The search results don't mark in-kind gifts; the state's downloadable year files do, and we used them.
  • Prices and terms. No report shows the rate, how many boards, how long, or what other candidates were offered.
  • Federal races and city races. We searched state filings only, not FEC records or city clerks' filings.
  • Other spellings. We found five. If a gift was filed under a spelling we didn't search, it isn't here.
  • Other billboard companies. This page covers Reagan. YESCO and Saunders Outdoor also appear in 2026 reports and are not counted here.

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

For billboard companies

  1. Do you offer every candidate the same rates and terms?
  2. What political ads have you declined, and why?
  3. When you give a campaign donated space, how is it valued and reported?

For your state legislators (le.utah.gov)

  1. Should billboard companies have an equal-access rule like broadcasters, or like Minnesota's?
  2. H.B. 33's first version required the "paid for by" line on ordinary signs to be readable up close. Why was that dropped?

For the Lieutenant Governor's Office (disclosures.utah.gov)

  1. Can the disclosure site show totals and group a company's different spellings together?
  2. The year files show whether a gift was cash or in-kind. Can the search results show it too?
  3. Who reviews mailers like the "10,000 Integrity Trackers" one that show no "paid for by" line?
  4. What happened to the 2024 complaints about Preserving Utah Values PAC after they were forwarded to the Attorney General?
  5. Has Roy City Citizens filed its report on the Prop. 10 mailer, and where can the public see it?

Check it yourself

  1. Go to disclosures.utah.gov and choose Advanced Search.
  2. Pick Contributions to see who a company gave to, or Expenditures to see who paid it. Pick the year, type the company name and click Find.
  3. Try every spelling. A "Reagan" search also picks up Reagan Airport travel and the unrelated Utah Reagan Caucus, so read each line.
  4. Click through every page of results. Totals are not shown; add them yourself.
  5. Look for the same name on both lists, and for gifts and payments on the same day.

How we checked

A tip came in with two tables: Reagan's 2026 gifts ($175,956, 64 rows) and payments to Reagan ($93,725.73). We searched disclosures.utah.gov Advanced Search for 2026 Contributions under "Reagan Outdoor," "Regan," "Reagan Inc" and "reagan outdoor," and 2026 Expenditures with payee "Reagan," and read every page. All 64 of the tip's gifts matched to the dollar. We found six more gifts filed under other spellings or filed later (Fiefia, Winterton, Albrecht, Plumb, Benson, Fitisemanu: $7,320). The tip's payment total, $93,725.73 in 36 entries, matched the state's search results. The state's downloadable 2026 year file, pulled the same day, includes 35 of those 36; Jill Koford's $5,630 payment dated Oct. 26 appears in the search results but not in the year file, so it may have been changed or is still being processed. We kept it in the total and note it here. two more payments filed to payee "Reagan" alone (Hollist, Lee: $1,990.10) are listed separately as unconfirmed. The tip's grand total of $269,681 added the two tables together; we keep them apart because one is gifts from Reagan and the other is payments to Reagan. We then downloaded the state's 2026 year files for candidates, PACs and political parties (100,202 transactions) and used them for cash vs. in-kind, the vendor totals in section 6 and the follow-up filings in section 5. We left out Reagan Airport travel, the Utah Reagan Caucus and people whose last name is Reagan.
