Who's Asking?

How polling changed, what's public on your Utah voter record, how campaigns find you and follow you, what the law allows, and what Utah's 2026 campaign reports show. From the public record.

Posted Nov. 17, 2026 · Updated Nov. 17, 2026 · Figures as of Nov. 17, 2026

In plain words

A poll used to be a phone call at dinner. Now it is more often a text with a link, or an online panel people join for reward points. Campaigns and vendors can use the same voter and commercial data for polls and for ads, texts and mailers.

In 2026 Utah made most voter records public again. Anyone can buy the statewide list for $1,050 unless a voter qualifies as "at-risk." Phone numbers and emails are not on that list, so campaigns match the list against other data to reach you.

What the records show: what's public and what's protected on your record, which laws apply to campaign calls, texts and email, and who Utah campaigns paid for polling, texting, data and digital ads in 2026. They don't show what any poll asked or who was targeted.

1. How polling changed

Pew Research Center studied national pollsters from 2000 to 2022. 61% of them used different methods in 2022 than in 2016, and 17% used three or more ways to reach people, up from 2% in 2016. Those count polling organizations. Counting individual polls instead, AAPOR estimated that 56% of national polls just before the 2016 election used online methods; for 2020 it was 84%.

A Utah example: same question, different groups. On Amendment B in 2026, Noble Predictive Insights' September poll surveyed 602 registered voters through an opt-in online panel (±3.99). Its Oct. 26–27 poll surveyed 527 likely voters by text-to-online (±4.27) and found 34% yes, 56% no and 10% unsure. The two polls reached different groups in different ways, so the change between them can't be credited entirely to voters changing their minds.

Online doesn't always mean random or not random. Probability-based online panels are recruited at random. Opt-in panels are not, so pollsters weight the answers to match the population. Weighting helps; it doesn't guarantee the sample represents everyone. For an opt-in survey, ask how its "plus or minus" was calculated; that figure doesn't necessarily measure selection bias, nonresponse or other errors.

2. A poll, a message test, or a "push poll"?

AAPOR calls a "push poll" negative campaigning disguised as a poll: political telemarketing to large numbers of people under the cover of research. Signs include only one or a few questions about one candidate or issue, uniformly negative (or positive) wording, no organization named or a fake one, and evasive answers. No single sign settles it. A message test is a real poll a campaign runs privately to see which arguments work; negative statements alone don't make it fake. Message tests usually run 400 to 1,500 interviews.

AAPOR also notes that legitimate polling firms will often choose not to identify the client who is sponsoring the research, because naming the sponsor could bias the answers. Utah law is different: since 2013 (H.B. 44, primary sponsor Rep. Greg Hughes), anyone who conducts a poll about a person who has filed for office, or a ballot proposition that has qualified for the ballot, must tell the person surveyed who paid for it, before or at the end. The lieutenant governor imposes a $100 fine for not doing so. In-person focus groups of 4 to 12 people are exempt, and so is a caller whose respondent ends the survey early (Utah Code 20A-11-905). Nothing is filed with the state, so the public can't check it afterward. A survey that ends with a donate button deserves a closer look at its purpose, but unpublished results alone don't make a poll fake.

3. Where they get your name

What's on your Utah voter record now

Public (anyone can buy, $1,050 statewide)
  • Full legal name
  • Voter ID number
  • Home and mailing address
  • Precinct and districts
  • Party
  • Active or inactive status
  • Date the record was last updated
  • Elections you voted in
Protected for every voter
  • Social Security number
  • Driver license or state ID number
  • Full date of birth
  • Email and phone number*
  • Signature
  • How you voted (the ballot is secret)

*Starting in 2027, the registration form asks whether you agree to share your phone and email with the party you're affiliated with. It's optional, and giving a phone or email at all is optional. The law limits the list to political purposes and makes knowingly posting it online a class A misdemeanor.

They can see that you voted, before Election Day. The state sells a "Who Has Voted" report for $35 per election. During regular primary and general elections it's emailed every day, starting 21 days before Election Day. It lists each voter whose ballot was processed, with name, address, party, how they voted (by mail or in person) and the date the ballot was processed. It never shows how you voted. Campaigns can use it to stop contacting people who already voted.

Counties sell voter data too. Sharon Bolos's 2026 Weber County Commission report lists $169.13 paid to Weber County for "voter data." The line doesn't say what the data included.

Where do phone numbers come from? Not from the state list. Campaigns and vendors match names and addresses against commercial data; Aristotle, a national vendor, advertises a Utah voter file with hundreds of thousands of cell numbers and emails attached. Numbers also come from anything you give a campaign: a petition, a sign-up, a donation, an RSVP. The Utah Republican Party's candidate page lists RNC voter data and a canvassing app for candidates who won at convention, and the party has announced a partnership with Right Impact, which it describes as a voter contact and data provider.

2018 · S.B. 74 (Sen. Karen Mayne, House sponsor Rep. Becky Edwards) and H.B. 218 (Rep. Rebecca Chavez-Houck, Senate sponsor Sen. Deidre Henderson) let any voter ask for a private record, for any reason. S.B. 74 also limited buyers to month and year of birth. Edwards and activist Ron Mortensen were the leading proponents.
July 31, 2019 · 206,832 of 1.67 million voters (12.4%) had chosen privacy. That September the Utah Republican and Democratic party chairs testified together asking lawmakers to change the law.
2020 · S.B. 83 (Sen. Jake Anderegg, House sponsor Rep. Brian King) let parties and candidates get certain information from private records and kept existing private records protected. It created a withholding request: domestic and dating violence victims could ask without extra proof; officers, service members, public figures and protective-order holders had to provide verification. Misuse became a class A misdemeanor with a possible civil fine.
2023 · H.B. 303 (Rep. Norm Thurston, Senate sponsor Anderegg), as enacted, let parties and candidates receive limited non-identifying data about withheld voters (a code from the voter ID and address, the address, party, precinct, voting history and an age range), with verification, use limits, security, penalties and notice. The privacy classification stayed in effect.
Feb. 27, 2025 · H.B. 270 (Rep. Trevor Lee), to make no-reason private records public, passes the House and does not become law that year.
March 2026 · S.B. 153 (Sen. John Johnson, R-North Ogden; House sponsor Rep. Trevor Lee) removes broad privacy protections, ending the "withheld" status used by nearly 300,000 voters and the "private" status used by about 1 million, while keeping and revising at-risk protections. Gov. Spencer Cox signs it in mid-March.
April–May 2026 · The Lieutenant Governor's Office notifies more than 300,000 voters; records become public May 25.
Nov. 18, 2026 · The Government Operations Interim Committee's agenda lists draft Voter Registration Records Amendments, which it says would change access to voter records and expand and modify at-risk eligibility.

  • Uploaded lists. Meta (Facebook and Instagram) still lets political advertisers upload lists and build "lookalike" audiences. Meta removed targeting by political affiliation and social-issue interests in early 2022.
  • Google and YouTube. Since late 2019, Google limits election ads to age, gender, general location (zip code) and context. It dropped voter-record and party targeting, list uploads and remarketing for election ads.
  • Streaming and web ads. In 2026 Rosemary Lesser's campaign reported paying StackAdapt, an ad-buying platform, $17,600 (Sept. 12 and 28).

6. Did you sign up for this? What the law says

Tap a card to flip it.

  • Calls and texts: campaign calls and texts are exempt from the National Do Not Call Registry. Under the federal Telephone Consumer Protection Act, autodialed or prerecorded political calls and autodialed texts to cell phones need prior consent.
  • Person-sent texts: in 2020 the FCC ruled that a peer-to-peer platform it reviewed, where a person hits send on each message, was not an autodialer. In 2021, in Facebook v. Duguid, the U.S. Supreme Court read "autodialer" narrowly. So some political texts fall outside the consent rule. Whether a system qualifies depends on its technology, not on whether a vendor calls it "peer-to-peer." An FCC public notice (DA 26-940) seeks comment on a petition to treat high-volume "click to send" systems as autodialers (read in search excerpts only).
  • Email: the federal CAN-SPAM Act covers commercial email. Noncommercial political messages fall outside it because of what they are, not who sends them.
  • "Paid for by": Utah's rule covers mailers, billboards, broadcast, print and "any other type of general public political advertising," but doesn't name texts or social media. See Case 18.
  • AI: since May 1, 2024, Utah political ads using AI-made images, video or audio must say so (S.B. 131).
The short answer: you can receive political outreach without having subscribed to that campaign. Being a registered voter puts your name, address, party and voting history on a list campaigns can buy. Phone numbers are matched from other sources. Some political texts and noncommercial political email don't require your consent.

7. How to tell if you're being targeted, and what you can do

  1. Facebook or Instagram: tap the three dots on an ad and choose "Why am I seeing this ad?" It shows some reasons, such as location or age, or that the advertiser used a list.
  2. Meta Ad Library (facebook.com/ads/library): search a candidate or group to see its political ads and available information on spending and who saw them. It doesn't fully explain why a particular person got an ad. Meta keeps political ads for seven years.
  3. Opt out of a list: Meta has a setting to stop seeing ads from an advertiser's custom audience from a list.
  4. Google Ads Transparency Center (adstransparency.google.com): search an advertiser's election ads.
  5. Texts: reply STOP. Forward unwanted texts to 7726 (SPAM). Report possible violations to the FCC at fcc.gov/complaints.
  6. Calls that sound like polls: ask which firm is conducting it and who paid for it. In Utah, a poll about a declared candidate or a qualified ballot measure must tell you who paid, before or at the end (Utah Code 20A-11-905).
  7. Your voter record: look up your registration at votesearch.utah.gov. You can apply to your county clerk for at-risk status at any time if you qualify.

10. An auditor's look

What the records let us confirm:

  • Who was paid, when and how much, for every payment filed with the state in 2026.
  • What the voter list contains, what it costs and who can buy it.
  • That the state sells a daily "Who Has Voted" file during elections.
  • What each voter-privacy law from 2018 to 2026 did, from the enacted texts.
  • UWHA's spending, from its own interim and final reports to the state.

What the records don't show, so we don't claim it:

  • What any poll asked, who was contacted, or whether results were released.
  • Who was targeted by any ad, text or mailer, or how lists were matched.
  • Whether any pollster shared respondents' answers with a campaign.
  • What Right Insight provided, or whether it is related to Right Impact.
  • Whether UWHA's ad-recruited survey answers were kept separate from its research sample, or reused.
  • What the Nov. 18 draft says line by line (its posted PDF text doesn't read cleanly); we quote the agenda's description.

Where the money is hard to follow: ad buying and polling often run through consultants; "purpose" lines are often just "campaign expense" or "advertising"; Utah's poll-sponsor disclosure is spoken during the poll, not filed; and UWHA's interim report shows a $62,500 payment we did not locate in its final report.
