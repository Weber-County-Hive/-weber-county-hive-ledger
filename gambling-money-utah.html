Casino Money and the Attorney General

Utah bans gambling. Gambling-industry companies still gave $115,800 to Utah candidates from 2018 to 2026. Here is every dollar, who got it, and what was happening at the time.

Posted Nov. 3, 2026 · Updated Nov. 3, 2026

New to this? Start with the easy version: short, simple words, no tables.Read the easy version →

In plain words

A tip came in about Attorney General Derek Brown taking money from gambling companies while his office charged one of them. We checked every candidate and PAC donation filed with the state from 2018 through 2026, 655,619 transactions in all.

What the records show: Brown received $83,000 from gambling-industry donors, more than every other Utah candidate in nine years combined ($32,800). Las Vegas Sands, a casino company that has long fought online gambling, gave only to Utah's attorneys general: $5,000 to Sean Reyes in 2018 and $50,000 to Brown on April 21, 2026, two months after Brown wrote that prediction markets are gambling and Kalshi sued the state. One sweepstakes-casino company, VGW, gave to 22 campaign accounts (21 legislators and Brown) in the two months before the Nov. 3, 2026 election.

What the records don't show: why anyone gave, or whether any donor asked for anything. Those are questions for readers to ask, listed at the bottom of this page.

Utah's rule on gambling

The Utah Constitution, Article VI, Section 27, says the Legislature "shall not authorize any game of chance, lottery or gift enterprise under any pretext or for any purpose." In 2026, H.B. 243 added "proposition bets" to the state's definition of gambling.

The companies on this page argue some of their products aren't gambling. Sweepstakes casinos and fantasy "pick'em" apps say they're games of skill or promotions; Kalshi says its bets are federally regulated futures contracts. Utah disagrees about Kalshi, and has sued and charged it.

Who gave, and who got sued

It's easy to read this as "took the money, then sued the givers." The records show something different:

  • Kalshi, the company Brown's office sued and charged, gave nothing to any Utah campaign, 2018–2026.
  • The companies that gave to Brown (Sands, DraftKings, PrizePicks, Arb Gaming, VGW and Stacie Stern) have not been sued or charged by his office, as far as we found. (VGW and Arb Gaming were named in private class-action suits in 2025; those are lawsuits by consumers, not the state.)
  • The biggest giver, Las Vegas Sands, is a competitor of online betting, not part of it. Its $50,000 came two months after Brown publicly called prediction markets gambling.
  • One donor changed what it does in Utah: On Nov. 2, after the Kalshi charges, DraftKings stopped offering its sports, novelty, political and some other event contracts (its prediction-market product) to people in Utah.

So the open question isn't "did he sue his donors." It's whether the companies that gave are being treated the same as the one that didn't. That's question 2 and 3 below.

Is this allowed?

Can gambling companies give to Utah candidates?

Yes. Utah has no limit on how much a person, company or union can give to a candidate for state office, and out-of-state companies can give. Every gift on this page was reported on the state's public disclosure site, as the law requires.

Can the attorney general take money from companies his office may look into?

We found no Utah law that stops it or requires the AG to step back from a case because of a donation. It has come up before: in 2008, then-AG Mark Shurtleff's campaign took $50,000 from Jeremy Johnson, head of iWorks, while Johnson was under investigation. Shurtleff said, "I never knew he was under investigation until now" (City Weekly). Whether Utah should have a rule is question 9 below.

Can I get in trouble for using these apps?

Utah law makes "participating in gambling," including online gambling, a class B misdemeanor (Utah Code 76-9-1402). Brown said on Oct. 28: "Gambling is illegal in Utah, whether you place the bet, run the game, or promote it." As far as we found, no Utah user has been charged; the charges so far are against Kalshi and its founders.

Is Brown on today's ballot?

No. Brown was elected Nov. 5, 2024, and took office in January 2025. Utah's attorney general serves a four-year term, so his next election is 2028.

The Utah way on gambling

  • Banned from the start. Utah's constitution, written in 1895 and in force at statehood in 1896, bans "any game of chance, lottery or gift enterprise." Utah has no casinos, no state lottery and no Powerball. Along with Hawaii, it has the strictest gambling prohibitions in the country (AP, March 2026).
  • Voters said no, too. A 1992 ballot measure to allow betting on horse races got about 40% and failed (Pignanelli, Deseret News, Oct. 7, 2026).
  • The faith of many Utahns. About half of Utahns belong to The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. At General Conference on Oct. 3, 2026, President D. Todd Christofferson said "gambling is morally wrong" and "built on the desire to obtain something for nothing," and spoke about the growth of prediction markets and sports betting (KUER).
  • Voices from both parties. Gov. Spencer Cox, as quoted by AP in March 2026: "We are putting a casino in the pocket of every single American, and they are targeting especially young people." Rep. Blake Moore introduced a federal bill to regulate prediction markets, and Sen. John Curtis cosponsored the Prediction Markets are Gambling Act (UPR, April 20, 2026). In a Deseret News opinion piece on Oct. 7, 2026, Republican political consultant and lobbyist Renae Cowley called gambling "a scourge upon humanity," and Frank Pignanelli, who served as a Democrat in the Utah Legislature, wrote that "issues, not wagers, should drive public policy."

That's the backdrop for the $115,800 in donations on this page.

Why do gambling companies give in a state that bans gambling? What each side says

Twenty of the 24 people who received this money are Republicans, and Utah's Constitution bans gambling. So why the money? No record shows anyone's reasons. But the public record does show the arguments on each side, and they help explain how this happens:

1. The companies say their products aren't gambling

  • Sweepstakes casinos (VGW): VGW's attorney, Bill Gantz, told Tennessee lawmakers on March 3, 2026, "This is not gambling," and said "no purchase is necessary to play a freemium game."
  • Pick'em apps (PrizePicks): PrizePicks told California's attorney general in 2024 that its contests are games of skill, where "skilled players achieve significantly more success."
  • Prediction markets (Kalshi): Kalshi says its bets are federally regulated financial contracts. It called Utah's charges a "crazy overreach" and said "Utah is clearly playing politics."

A lawmaker who accepts those arguments may not see a gift from these companies as conflicting with the gambling ban. Utah's answer, in H.B. 243 and in court, is that they are gambling.

2. Some see it as a federal question

The federal Commodity Futures Trading Commission says only it can regulate prediction markets, and it sued Utah on Oct. 27, 2026. Its chair said Congress made the CFTC "the federal regulator with sole responsibility to oversee these markets." Utah says its constitution decides what is gambling here.

3. Some worry about going too far

When the House debated H.B. 243, Rep. Matt MacPherson asked whether it would reach "player-to-player fantasy-type engagements where it's more based on skill." The sponsor, Rep. Joseph Elison, said it would not. Nine House members voted no. Their reasons aren't in the news coverage we found.

4. The gifts came after the vote

Seventeen of the 21 legislators VGW gave to voted for the ban on prop bets, months before VGW's gifts. During the House debate, Rep. Tyler Clancy, who received no gambling money, said: "Sports gambling is destroying my generation." Utah has no limit on campaign gifts.

What's still unknown: why these companies give to people who keep gambling illegal, and what, if anything, they expect. Those are questions 7, 12, 13 and 14 below.

What Kalshi is charged with

  • Criminal case (filed Oct. 28, 2026, Salt Lake City): Kalshi and co-founders Tarek Mansour and Luana Lopes Lara face 14 counts: one count of pattern of unlawful activity (second-degree felony), 12 counts of online gambling promotion (third-degree felonies) and one count of gambling promotion (class A misdemeanor). These are charges, not convictions. No court date was reported.
  • Civil case (filed Oct. 20, 2026): The state asks the court to stop Kalshi from operating in Utah and estimates Kalshi collected $30.6 million to $61.2 million in fees from Utah accounts through Oct. 19, 2026 (the state's estimate, per The Block and Covers).
  • The federal side: A federal judge ruled for Utah in August; the 10th Circuit refused to pause that on Sept. 8; and on Oct. 27 the CFTC sued Utah and Brown, saying federal law, not Utah's, covers these markets.

The Attorney General's connections

  • Lincoln Hill. Utah's corporate registry lists Derek Brown as a director of Lincoln Hill PAC, with Casey Hill and Lincoln Shurtz. Brown's company, Integrity Strategies LLC, gave that PAC $17,500, and the PAC gave "Derek Brown for Attorney General" $15,100. That $15,100 includes $10,000 dated Sept. 24, 2025 (Brown's 2025 report, filed under the name Lincoln Hill Partners PAC).
  • Lobbying. A "Brown, Derek" was a registered Utah lobbyist from 2017 to 2023 (lobbyist.utah.gov, record now closed). His 54 listed clients include no gambling or prediction-market company.
  • Kalshi's lobbyist. Chase Everton, who joined Lincoln Hill Partners with Brown in the 2021 merger, is Kalshi's only registered Utah lobbyist (since July 30, 2026).
  • VGW's lobbyist. Lincoln Shurtz of Lincoln Hill Partners has been VGW's registered Utah lobbyist since Jan. 20, 2026.
  • Campaign vendor. Brown's campaign paid Election Hive $68,212 in 2025. The Deseret News quotes its founder, Matt Lusty, as Brown's campaign advisor.
For fairness: Brown's office is suing and prosecuting Kalshi, the company his former colleague at Lincoln Hill Partners lobbies for. Lusty told the Deseret News the donations "have no influence on his decision-making." Las Vegas Sands has a long public record of opposing online gambling, so its money may line up with the AG's fight against prediction markets rather than against it. No record shows why any donor gave.

What this search can't see

  • Money passed through national groups. Brown's 2024 campaign reported $50,000 from the "Republican National Attorneys General Association" (as filed) on Aug. 29, 2024. Utah's records don't show who gave that group its money, so any gambling money inside it wouldn't show up here.
  • Money under other names. We searched donor names. A gift from a company's employee, lobbyist or another business name wouldn't show up unless the name points to gambling.
  • Party and federal money. We didn't search Utah political-party filings or federal (FEC) records.

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

For AG Derek Brown's office (attorneygeneral.utah.gov)

  1. Las Vegas Sands gave your campaign $50,000 on April 21, 2026, two months after your Feb. 22 op-ed calling prediction markets gambling. Did anyone from Sands contact your office about Kalshi or prediction markets before or after that donation?
  2. You said the Kalshi case is "part of a larger investigation," and The Block reported you said Utah is focused on Kalshi for now (Oct. 20, 2026). Are the other companies that gave to your campaign part of that investigation: DraftKings, PrizePicks, Underdog, Arb Gaming and VGW?
  3. Do DraftKings, PrizePicks, Underdog, Arb Gaming or VGW currently take money from people in Utah? If so, why haven't they been charged the way Kalshi was?
  4. Kalshi's only registered Utah lobbyist, Chase Everton, joined Lincoln Hill Partners with you in 2021. Have you stepped back from any part of the Kalshi case because of that history?
  5. Will your campaign keep or return the gambling-industry donations?
  6. The national attorneys general association that gave your campaign $50,000 in August 2024 doesn't list its donors in Utah records. Did any gambling-industry money reach your campaign through it?

For your state legislators (le.utah.gov)

  1. VGW Luckyland, a sweepstakes-casino company named in Utah class-action suits, gave to 21 legislators between Aug. 31 and Oct. 29, 2026. Did you receive any of it, and will you vote on sweepstakes or gambling bills in 2027?
  2. Utah's Constitution bans games of chance. Does Utah law clearly cover sweepstakes casinos, fantasy pick'em apps and prediction markets, or does the Legislature need to close gaps?
  3. Utah has no limit on campaign gifts and no rule that keeps an attorney general off cases involving donors. Should there be one?
  4. For the nine House members who voted no on H.B. 243, including Reps. Candice Pierucci and Jordan Teuscher: why did you vote against it?

For Gov. Spencer Cox's office (governor.utah.gov)

  1. The American Gaming Association, the casino industry's trade group, gave your campaign $2,500 in April 2025. What position has your office taken on prediction markets like Kalshi, which sued you and the AG?

For the companies

  1. Las Vegas Sands: why give only to Utah's attorneys general, Reyes in 2018 and Brown in 2026, and no other Utah candidates?
  2. VGW: why give to 22 Utah campaign accounts in the two months before the Nov. 3, 2026 election?
  3. PrizePicks: why give to both of Utah's last two attorneys general (Reyes in 2023, if Performance Predictions LLC is PrizePicks, and Brown in 2024 and 2025)? And Stacie Stern: are the 2024 gift (Brooklyn address) and the 2026 gift (Rolling Hills address) from the same person, and were either given on behalf of Underdog?

For the Lt. Governor's Office (disclosures.utah.gov)

  1. Why does the state's bulk data list many of Brown's 2025 donations twice, under different transaction numbers (including PrizePicks and DraftKings), when his filed report shows each once?

How we checked

We downloaded the state's "Download Data by Year" files for Candidates & Office Holders and for Political Action Committees, every year from 2018 to 2026 (18 files, 655,619 transactions), and searched every donor name for gambling, sweepstakes, fantasy-sports and prediction-market companies, plus the words "gaming," "casino," "betting" and "sweepstake." We removed false matches (a pizza chain, a person named Bettinger, campaign hotel bills). We confirmed Brown's donations on his own filed reports. This is a donor-name search: money given through people or firms with other names would not appear. Political party filings were not searched.
