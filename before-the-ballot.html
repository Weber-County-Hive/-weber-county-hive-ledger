The Ledger · Case 20 · Candidate Recruiting and Signature Money

Before the Ballot

Who recruits Utah candidates, and who pays to get them on the ballot. What Utah's 2026 campaign reports show about signature gathering, recruiting groups and the money behind them, from the public record.

Posted Nov. 17, 2026 · Updated Nov. 17, 2026 · Figures as of Nov. 17, 2026

New to this? Start with the easy version: short, simple words, no tables.Read the easy version →

In plain words

By the time you see a candidate's name on a mailer or a ballot, two things have already happened. Someone decided to run, often after being asked. And that person got onto the ballot, either by winning delegates at a party convention or by turning in voter signatures.

In 2026, many campaigns paid companies to gather those signatures. Some PACs paid for signatures for candidates they chose to help. Groups on both sides say they recruit candidates, but no report shows who asked whom to run.

What the records show: who paid for signature gathering in 2026, how much, under what names, and whether those candidates reached their signature count. They also show where some recruiting groups' money came from. They don't show who recruited whom, or what each payment bought.

1. Two doors to a party's primary ballot

For most of Utah's history, a party's nominee was chosen by delegates at a convention. In 2014, a citizen initiative called Count My Vote pushed for direct primaries. Lawmakers answered with a compromise, S.B. 54, which Gov. Gary Herbert signed on March 10, 2014. It kept conventions and gave members seeking a qualified party's nomination an additional route to the primary ballot: gather enough signatures from voters in their own party.

Candidates can take either door, or both. The number of signatures depends on the office (Utah Code 20A-9-408). In 2026 it was 1,000 for a state House seat and 2,000 for a state Senate seat. County clerks check every signature, and any voter can look up the petitions they signed in the past six months at vote.utah.gov.

2. Who asks people to run

Some Utah groups say plainly that finding candidates is part of what they do. Tap a card to see what each says and what its 2026 reports show.

Tap a card to flip it. Group descriptions come from each group's own website or from the news report cited.

One recruiting effort was announced publicly. On July 29, 2025, KSL reported that Vote Save America, the political arm of Crooked Media, would spend $20,000 to partner with Utah's Elevate PAC to find and recruit candidates. The targets were eight Republican House members and two Republican senators: Jill Koford, Matt MacPherson, Anthony Loubet, Jim Dunnigan, Ken Ivory, Clint Okerlund, Steve Eliason, Jordan Teuscher, Dan McCay and Kirk Cullimore.

The same races drew organized help from more than one direction. Jim Dunnigan, for example, received CMV PAC signature support ($5,625), was on the Elevate recruiting target list, and was supported by a Utah Doers PAC independent expenditure. Each is a separate record.

A website tells you what a group says it does. It doesn't tell you whom it asked. Campaign finance reports don't have a field for who encouraged someone to run. Money or donated services may show up later, but a contribution alone doesn't prove a group recruited anyone.

Candidates and groups reported money from "Elevate PAC." Here is how the names fit together:

  • The PAC's state filing (its Statement of Organization) names it "Elevate PAC." It was created May 3, 2022, and lists no other name.
  • Its report for Sept. 26 to Oct. 22 is also filed as "Elevate PAC," with a Salt Lake City address.
  • The state's downloadable 2026 data lists the same reports as "Life Elevated."
  • The website elevateutahpac.com says it is "Paid for by Elevate Utah PAC." Its Donate button goes to an ActBlue page for "Elevate PAC."
  • KSL listed an elevateutahpac.com email as the contact for Elevate PAC's recruiting.
  • The PAC reported $30,270.25 from "Substack." Elevate runs a paid Substack newsletter. It also paid Squarespace, ActBlue and ShipStation.

What this means: these records point to one committee using three names.

Who runs it. The state filing lists Gabrielle Finlayson (president), Jacqueline Morgan (vice president) and Benjamin Haynes (treasurer). All three list their job as consultant. All three use email addresses at elevatecampaignstrategies.com and the PAC's Salt Lake City suite.

The PAC and the consulting firm. Elevate PAC paid Elevate Strategies $25,000.14 in 2026. Elevate Strategies was paid $520,686.71 by 18 filers in all. KUER called Gabi Finlayson president of Elevate Utah PAC and a senior partner at Elevate Strategies. The Salt Lake Tribune called the PAC the political action committee for Elevate Strategies.

What this means: the PAC and the firm share people and an address. That doesn't show who owns the firm.

A payment to the president. On June 12, the PAC reported a $10,000 "Campaign Expense" payment to Gabrielle Finlayson, its president. The report doesn't say what it covered.

Where Elevate PAC's money went, and came from

Elevate PAC, 2026 through mid-November: $745,482.12 in contributions and $583,693.74 in spending, from the state year file. Its Oct. 22 report shows $742,755.93 in contributions for the year to that date.

Who is The Frosty Group?

May 13, 2026 · Aaron Frost signs Articles of Incorporation for Frosty Group, Inc. as incorporator. The Articles are stamped "Expedite" and were drafted by the national law firm Foley & Lardner LLP's Milwaukee office.
May 14, 2026 · Filed at 2:12 p.m. A Utah corporation in Draper; registered agent CT Corporation System; may engage in "any lawful act or activity"; 1,000 shares. No owners are named.
Aug. 5, 2026 · Curiosity Holdings, LLC is formed with the same registered agent (Aaron Frost) and the same Draper address. Its record does not list owners.
Oct. 19, 2026 · Elevate PAC reports $400,000 from "The Frosty Group" of Draper, at the same street address listed as Frosty Group, Inc.'s principal office.

The matching address ties the donor to Frosty Group, Inc. The Articles don't name the owners or say where the money came from. Curiosity Holdings does not appear in the 2026 campaign finance data.

What this means: the records show who gave the $400,000: a five-month-old corporation. They don't show who owns it.

A caution on names. An Aaron Frost, known as "Frosty," founded the Utah software company HeroDevs and was its CEO until the end of 2025. Utah business records also list an Aaron Frost as a director of the 2019 nonprofit "Joel Frost for Sandy City Council," and more than one person whose name includes "Aaron Frost." A matching name, nickname or city is not enough to identify a donor. No record we found states that Frosty Group, Inc.'s incorporator is the HeroDevs founder or the nonprofit's director. We report the corporation and its listed incorporator only. Who owns or controls Frosty Group, Inc., and where its $400,000 came from, are not shown in the records we reviewed.

5. When a PAC pays for the signatures

Several PACs and a party committee reported paying for candidates' signature gathering themselves, not just giving money.

CMV PAC: 16 candidates, Jan. 19 to Feb. 17. Most entries were $5,625; Scott Sandall, Tamara Tran and Brian McKenzie each got $11,250 and Corey Astill $16,875. Utah Association of Realtors: in-kind through AC Strategies for Chris Sloan ($21,250) and Stephanie Bagwell ($4,250), April 15. Checks & Balances PAC: through Summit Civic Strategies and Empower Public Affairs. Utah Republican Senate Campaign Committee: Gathering, Inc., May 11. Fresh Air PAC ($9,250 to Apollo Political) and Governing Group PAC ($13,396.08 to individual gatherers) are not shown because their reports don't say which petition the work was for.

Who runs CMV PAC. Its state filing shows:

  • Created Jan. 15, 2016
  • Other name it uses: "Count My Vote"
  • Treasurer: Val Oveson, an accountant
  • Directors: Rich McKeown and Taylor Morgan
  • Officer: David May

Count My Vote was also the name of the 2014 initiative. We have not documented whether the 2016 committee is the same group.

A clue from the lobbyist records. Taylor Morgan is a registered lobbyist with Morgan May Public Affairs. His clients include "Alliance for Good Government Political Action Committee (Count My Vote)," from March 5, 2014, to Jan. 18, 2018. Then CMV PAC starts Jan. 18, 2018, the same day. It's listed at the same Draper address as his firm, which is also CMV PAC's address. David May, of the same firm and a CMV PAC officer, has listed "Count My Vote" as a client since Jan. 4, 2021.

What this means: the same people run CMV PAC and represent Count My Vote. That doesn't prove the two committees are one group.

Where CMV PAC's money came from. In 2026 it took in $412,055.40.

  • $361,000 came from "Utah Legacy Endowed Fund," in three gifts (Jan. 14, March 12 and May 28). The donor's address was in Cedar City.
  • $50,000 came from a donor filed as Scott Anderson of Salt Lake City, on Jan. 27. We have not confirmed which Scott Anderson.

Is the fund the one in the SEC filing? A federal SEC filing is for Utah Legacy Endowed Fund, LLC, a Delaware company with a Salt Lake City office. Its manager is Legacy Investment Partners LLC. Michael Okerlund Leavitt and Gail Miller are listed as members of the manager. The names match, but the addresses don't, so we have not matched the donor to that company. This is a lead, not a finding.

Not on the corporation list. Neither Utah Legacy Endowed Fund nor Frosty Group appears on the state's list of corporations that file political-spending reports (checked Nov. 17, 2026, under F, T and U). Since May 12, 2015, a corporation doesn't have to report money it gives to a committee that reports it.

One benefit, two entries. When a PAC pays for a candidate's signatures, the state's PAC guide tells the PAC to report "the entity to whom the expenditure was ultimately disbursed" and "the recipient of the in-kind contribution." The candidate may also report it as an in-kind gift. Those are two descriptions of the same help, so we count each once.

Campaigns also paid other signature firms directly. The largest was Gathering Inc. (also filed as "Gather"): $72,548.25 from 10 campaigns. It also appears in Case 18.

8. Where it happens

Pins: the State Capitol (where S.B. 54, H.B. 32 and S.B. 194 passed, and where the Lieutenant Governor's Office keeps the signature totals and disclosure reports); Elevate PAC's address on its state report; and the Weber and Davis county clerks, who check candidate signatures. We do not map private addresses.

9. What the Legislature did, and didn't do

H.B. 32 (2026), Signature Gathering and Verification Amendments. Primary sponsor: Rep. Paul Cutler. Signed March 23.

  • Starting May 6, anyone collecting petition signatures must first take the state's online training. That includes candidate petitions.
  • People who carry candidate petitions may be paid by the hour or per valid signature.
  • Starting July 1, 2027, signers get an email or text notice.

Every payment in the section 4 table is dated January through March, before the training rule began.

S.B. 194 (2026), Election Modifications. Primary sponsor: Sen. Mike McKell. Signed March 23. A county clerk running for reelection must hand signature checking to another county's clerk.

H.B. 95 (2025), Financial Disclosure Revisions. Primary sponsor: Rep. Michael J. Petersen. In effect since Jan. 1, 2026. The state's disclosure website must let anyone search every filer for one person's contributions or spending.

Five 2026 bills failed: H.B. 25, H.B. 27, H.B. 167, H.B. 223 and H.B. 460. Their verdicts are in the table below.

10. An auditor's look

What the records let us confirm:

  • Every payment and in-kind entry on this page, as filed, with date, amount, name and purpose.
  • Each In The Field client's office and party, from the state's candidate lists.
  • What each recruiting group says it does, from its own website.
  • Elevate PAC's and CMV PAC's officers, from their Statements of Organization.
  • That the Elevate PAC donor "The Frosty Group" matches Frosty Group, Inc.'s principal-office address.
  • The final action on each bill, from the Legislature's records.

What the records don't show, so we don't claim it:

  • Who encouraged any candidate to run.
  • How many signatures each payment bought, how many were valid, and the price per signature.
  • What the "Campaign Expense" and "Advertising" payments to In The Field covered.
  • Who owns or funds Frosty Group, Inc. or Curiosity Holdings, LLC.
  • Whether the "Utah Legacy Endowed Fund" on CMV PAC's reports is the LLC in the SEC filing.

Where it's hard to follow: one company appears under at least six spellings; one PAC files as "Elevate PAC" but the year file lists it as "Life Elevated"; payments to the same vendor appear under different purposes; and donated signature work shows up twice, sometimes on different dates.

For fairness: Paying people to gather signatures is legal in Utah, and H.B. 32 now says candidate-petition circulators may be paid hourly or per valid signature. PACs may pay for help a candidate receives, if it's reported. Recruiting candidates is legal and common across parties. The transactions here do not, by themselves, establish wrongdoing.

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

For the Lieutenant Governor's Office (vote.utah.gov)

  1. How many valid signatures did each 2026 signature candidate submit, and who gathered them?
  2. Could reports list each vendor under one standard name? Why does the downloadable year file list Elevate PAC as "Life Elevated"?

For CMV PAC

  1. How did you choose the 16 candidates whose signature gathering you paid for in January and February 2026?

For Elevate PAC

  1. Is the committee on file with the state also the one that runs elevateutahpac.com, and does it use the name "Life Elevated"?

For campaigns that filed "Campaign Expense" or "Advertising"

  1. What did the In The Field payment cover?

For recruiting groups

  1. Whom did you encourage to run in 2026, and did you pay for their signatures or consultants?

For legislators (le.utah.gov)

  1. Should paid signature help from a PAC be reported the same way in both reports? Should corporate donors have to show who owns them?

Check it yourself

  1. See which petitions you signed: vote.utah.gov (past six months).

  3. See a campaign's payments: disclosures.utah.gov, Advanced Search, Expenditures, then search "In the Field," "Black Peak" or "Gathering." Try every spelling.
  4. See Elevate PAC's October report: report 207607.
