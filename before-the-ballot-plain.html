Before the Ballot

The short, simple version. No tables.

Posted Nov. 17, 2026 · Updated Nov. 17, 2026

Want every number and every source?Read the full case file →

The big idea

Before you ever vote, two things happen. Someone decides to run, often because a group asked them. Then they get on the ballot, by winning delegates at a convention or by turning in voter signatures. In 2026, a lot of money paid for those signatures.

Two ways onto a party's ballot

  • Convention: party delegates vote.
  • Signatures: since a 2014 law (S.B. 54), voters in the party can sign a petition instead. In 2026 it took 1,000 signatures for a state House seat and 2,000 for a state Senate seat.
  • A candidate can try both.

Who paid for signatures

$118,125One PAC, CMV PAC, paid for signature gathering for 16 candidates
15 of 24Candidates with paid help who got enough signatures
  • Many campaigns paid companies to gather signatures. One company, In The Field (also called Black Peak), worked for 11 campaigns in both parties. Its name was spelled at least six different ways in the reports.
  • Some PACs paid for signatures for candidates they chose. The same help can show up twice: once on the PAC's report and once on the candidate's.
  • Paying didn't always work. 9 of 24 candidates came up short. Many of them still had the convention.

Who asks people to run

  • Groups on both sides say they recruit candidates, like Republican Roundtable PAC, Salt Lake County Democrats and Elevate PAC.
  • No report shows who asked whom to run.
  • Elevate PAC goes by three names: Elevate PAC, Elevate Utah PAC, and "Life Elevated" in the state's download.
  • Elevate PAC got $400,000 on Oct. 19, 2026, from The Frosty Group, a Draper company formed five months earlier. The records don't show who owns it.

What the Legislature did

  • Passed in 2026: people who gather signatures must take state training first (H.B. 32). A county clerk running for reelection can't check signatures in their own race (S.B. 194).
  • Passed in 2025: the state website must let you search every report for one person's donations (H.B. 95).
  • Failed in 2026: five bills, including ones to post signers' names online.
  • No law yet: reports don't show who recruited a candidate, or who owns a company that donates.

Our grade: C

The money we looked at was reported, which meets the basic rule. But voters can't see who recruited a candidate, what each payment bought, or who is behind a company that gives.

What you can do

  • See which petitions you signed at vote.utah.gov.
  • Look up any campaign's money at disclosures.utah.gov.
  • Ask your legislator: should reports show who owns a company that donates?
