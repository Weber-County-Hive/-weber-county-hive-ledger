Why the total is $17.80 million, not $19.4 million

Some donors on this page gave money to other donors on this page. When that happens, the same dollars show up twice: once when the first donor gives them, and again when the second donor spends them.

The money did not disappear. Every one of those dollars is still shown on this page, in full, on both donors' tiles and flow lines. We just don't add the same dollars into the combined total two times.

Example: RPAC gave the Salt Lake Board of Realtors $908,030. The Salt Lake Board then spent that money.

That $908,030 is inside RPAC's total (money RPAC gave) and inside the Salt Lake Board's total (money the Board spent). Adding both totals would count it twice, so the combined total counts it once.

It works like a paycheck: if your boss pays you $100 and you pay $100 in rent, there's still only $100, not $200.

Conclusion & questions readers should ask

This page documents about $17.80 million in giving across 23 companies, PACs, and individuals, counted once*, and the people who run the PACs receiving and passing that money on. (Every donor's total added together is $19.4 million; about $1.63 million of that went from one donor on this page to another, so it is counted one time. See how the total adds up.) A few patterns show up repeatedly across otherwise unrelated donors: the same handful of names (Kyle Palmer, Matthew Lusty, Lincoln Shurtz, Casey Hill, Mike Ostermiller, Chris Kyler) sit as officers, treasurers, or paid vendors across multiple PACs at once; PACs frequently pay their own officers or leadership directly; at least one official in a leadership PAC role (Speaker Schultz) loaned his own campaign a large personal sum; and Utah's largest PACs (RPAC, UHREC, NUPAC) redistribute money down through a dense network of sub-PACs, local boards, and vendors rather than giving directly to candidates. 2026 figures throughout are incomplete, since the reporting year isn't over.

Questions worth asking, based on what's documented here:

  • Why do so many of Utah's largest PACs share the same handful of treasurers, officers, and paid vendors?
  • When a PAC pays its own officer or leadership directly (as several here do), what oversight exists to confirm that payment reflects fair value for services rendered?
  • What explains an official lending a large personal sum to his own campaign in the same period his leadership PACs are raising institutional money?
  • When a PAC's money moves through several layers — parent org to state PAC to local board to candidate — at what point, if any, does a donor's original intent stop being traceable?
  • Do the industries giving the most (real estate, energy, banking) correspond to the committees and bills their recipients sit on or sponsor?
  • Why do so many entities on this page show [DELETED] entries in their own filed reports, and what governs when a deletion needs a public explanation?

These are open questions the public record raises, not conclusions this page draws. Readers should look at the primary sources linked throughout and decide for themselves what the documented facts mean.

Sources & methodology
  • Itemized Utah campaign-finance disclosures, disclosures.utah.gov — individual contribution/expenditure searches and full PAC DEX (CSV) exports
  • Utah lobbyist registration records, disclosures.utah.gov and lobbyist.utah.gov — Statements of Organization and filed client/principal reports
  • Sworn Conflict of Interest / Annual Financial Disclosure forms filed directly by officeholders, disclosures.utah.gov (Utah Code §20A-11-1604 and §10-3-1313)
  • Utah Division of Corporations business-entity records (business registry, officer/manager filings) and opencorporates.com cross-references
  • Utah's boards and commissions registry, boards.utah.gov — appointing authority and seat-qualification records
  • Utah Code and legislative bill text, le.utah.gov — direct statute citations rather than secondhand summaries
  • Nonprofit Form 990 filings via ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer
  • IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search — 501(c)(3) status verification
  • Better Business Bureau business profiles — independent verification of business ownership/operating status
  • Weber County Commission minutes and other public meeting records
  • LinkedIn — individuals' own posted work histories, current roles, and self-reported client lists
  • Firms' own public-facing bios, "in the news" pages, and press releases (Legislative Insight Consulting, Lincoln Hill Partners, Election Hive)
  • Wikipedia and contemporaneous news archives (KSL, AP, Deseret News) — biographical and historical confirmation
  • Salt Lake Tribune, Threads post, 5/19/2026
  • Wayback Machine (web.archive.org) — an archived Election Hive bio page no longer live on the current site
